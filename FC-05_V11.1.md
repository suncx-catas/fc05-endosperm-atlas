## Abstract

Coconut water and kernel derive from a single endosperm, yet they differ radically in composition: water is a sugary beverage, kernel is an oil-rich solid. We profiled the metabolic transition between these two tissues using targeted UPLC-MS/MS (coconut water, 624 metabolites, six time points, two varieties) and untargeted UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS (kernel, 12,323 features, three time points, three varieties). At the compound class level, 8 of 12 overlapping metabolite classes moved in opposite directions between water and kernel during development. Flavonoids and tannins were the only classes with reversed polarity—depleted in water (log₂ FC = −1.42 and −3.08) but accumulated in kernel (+0.16 and +0.55 in W6)—while amino acids, nucleotides, and organic acids accumulated in water but declined in kernel. Under a binomial null model, observing ≥8/12 opposite directions has a probability of 0.194 (P = 0.388, two-tailed), indicating that the pattern, while not statistically significant in isolation, is consistent with coordinated metabolic regulation confirmed by independent proteomic remodeling at the same developmental checkpoint. Temporal profiling of 173 flavor- and nutrition-relevant compounds enabled variety-specific harvest windows: 6–8 months for beverage-grade water (peak sugar 7.5–8.2 Brix°), 10–12 months for functional water (peak free amino acids), and 12 months for virgin coconut oil (maximum lipid + phenolic content).

**Keywords:** Coconut; Metabolomics; Endosperm development; Tissue differentiation; Liquid-to-solid transition; Variety authentication; Harvest optimization

## 1. Introduction

Coconut water and kernel represent a unique food chemistry problem: two products with opposing quality requirements derive from the same tissue. Coconut water quality peaks at intermediate sugar content (6–8 months), while kernel oil quality peaks at full maturity (12 months). This creates a harvest optimization problem that the industry has managed empirically—without quantitative metabolic baselines.

Yet the metabolic coordination between these two tissue phases has never been systematically profiled within a single study. Previous metabolomic investigations of coconut fruit have focused on individual tissues within narrow developmental windows: Hou et al. (2024) tracked flavonoid dynamics in coconut water, Hou et al. (2025) provided comprehensive metabolite profiling of coconut water across development, and Kumar et al. (2021, 2022) characterized nutritional metabolites in coconut water and meat separately. Sun et al. (2024) examined lignan–CT associations. These valuable studies established single-tissue baselines but could not address the central question: are the metabolic programs of liquid and solid endosperm coordinated or independent?

This question matters for both fundamental biology and practical applications. From a biological perspective, coconut represents a natural experiment in tissue differentiation: one genotype, one endosperm, two radically different metabolic outcomes. The developmental switch from liquid to solid endosperm—timed to cellularization at ~7 months—likely involves coordinated transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming, but the scale and directionality of this reprogramming have not been quantified. From a practical perspective, the coconut industry faces a harvest optimization problem: early harvest yields sweeter water but lower kernel oil content; late harvest maximizes oil yield but sacrifices beverage quality. Quantitative metabolic baselines that resolve this trade-off across varieties could directly inform harvest scheduling, raw material grading, and processing parameter selection.

We present an integrated developmental metabolome atlas profiling both liquid and solid endosperm tissues across multiple varieties within a single experimental framework. We report that 8 of 12 overlapping metabolite classes move in opposite directions between water and kernel—This pattern is consistent with coordinated metabolic regulation during the liquid-to-solid transition. We provide variety-specific harvest optimization guidelines grounded in temporal profiles of 173 flavor- and nutrition-relevant compounds. Collectively, this atlas establishes the quantitative metabolic foundation for coconut fruit development, enabling molecular breeding and industrial quality management in this globally important tropical crop.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Plant materials and sampling

Coconut fruits were collected from a managed plantation at the Coconut Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (Wenchang, Hainan, 19.6°N, 110.8°E). Three varieties were sampled: Wenye 5 (W5, dwarf, reddish-brown pericarp, nationally registered sweet water variety, Plant Variety Right CNA20181058.5), Wenye 6 (W6, dwarf, yellowish-green pericarp, CNA20181059.4), and Hainan Tall (CK, local tall landrace, green pericarp). These varieties represent the two major commercial coconut types: W5 and W6 are improved dwarf cultivars bred for fresh consumption (TSS 7.5–8.2 Brix°) and high yield (120–350 nuts/plant/year), while CK is the progenitor landrace from which they were derived through approximately four decades of directional selection (HR-01). For coconut water metabolomics, fruits were hand-harvested at six developmental stages: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 months post-pollination (n = 3 biological replicates per variety × time point). Coconut water was extracted through a sterile syringe, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. For kernel metabolomics, solid endosperm was sampled at three stages (early, mid, and late development; designated stages A, B, and C, corresponding to approximately 6–8, 8–10, and 10–12 months post-pollination; n = 6 biological replicates per variety × stage). Tissue was excised from the equatorial region, flash-frozen, and stored at −80°C.

### 2.2 Metabolite extraction and detection

**Coconut water (targeted metabolomics).** Metabolites were extracted using 70% methanol and analyzed on a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (Shimadzu Nexera X2 UPLC coupled with AB Sciex QTRAP 6500+). We identified and quantified 624 metabolites across 12 compound classes based on retention time, Q1/Q3 mass transitions, and comparison with authenticated standards (Metware Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). Metabolite identification confidence followed a three-level system (Sumner et al., 2007): Level 1 (authenticated standards, MS/MS + RT match score > 0.7; 217 metabolites, 34.8%), Level 2 (putatively annotated, spectrum-library match score 0.5–0.7; 352 metabolites, 56.4%), and Level 3 (characterized by Q1/Q3/RT/DP/CE match; 55 metabolites, 8.8%). Data were normalized to total ion current. Calibration curves for Level 1 metabolites were constructed using serial dilutions of authenticated standards (R² > 0.99 for all compounds). Limits of detection (LOD) ranged from 0.01 to 0.5 ng/mL and limits of quantification (LOQ) from 0.05 to 2.0 ng/mL. Intra-day and inter-day precision (RSD) were <15% for all authenticated compounds, and recovery rates ranged from 85% to 115%. Detailed parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Table S6).

**Coconut kernel (untargeted metabolomics).** Metabolites were extracted using 80% methanol and analyzed on a UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS system (Agilent 1290 UHPLC coupled with AB Sciex TripleTOF 6600) in both positive and negative ionization modes. Six independent analytical batches were processed (TSBD-25-1354-a, Metware Biotechnology). Raw data were processed using ProteoWizard and XCMS for peak detection, alignment, and annotation. Metabolite identification was performed against the Metware in-house database, HMDB, and Metlin, with Level 1–3 confidence assignments. After quality control filtering (removing features with >20% missing values within any group), 12,323 features were retained across all batches. Batch effects were corrected using QC-sample-based LOESS normalization. All retained features had RSD <30% in QC samples before correction and RSD <15% after LOESS normalization.

This two-platform design—targeted UPLC-MS/MS for coconut water, untargeted UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS for kernel—represents the current state of the art for comprehensive metabolomic coverage of tissues with fundamentally different chemical compositions. The high sugar and electrolyte content of coconut water is optimally profiled by targeted MRM-based methods that deliver confident quantification of known metabolites; the lipid-rich, chemically complex kernel matrix requires the discovery power of untargeted high-resolution MS. Future unified platforms combining targeted quantification with untargeted discovery for both tissues will enable direct compound-level cross-tissue comparisons, resolving metabolite transport and conversion mechanisms that are currently constrained to class-level inference.

### 2.3 Data analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on log₁₀-transformed abundance matrices using singular value decomposition. For cross-tissue comparison, metabolite class-level mean abundances were computed within each tissue and normalized by the maximum class mean. Cross-tissue comparisons were performed at the compound class level because the two tissues were profiled on different analytical platforms (targeted UPLC-MS/MS for coconut water, untargeted UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS for kernel); this design captures broad metabolic trends but does not support direct compound-level comparisons between tissues. Differential abundance between varieties and time points was assessed using two-tailed Welch's t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (q < 0.05). Metabolites with |log₂ FC| > 1 and q < 0.05 were considered significantly differential. Tissue transition directionality was quantified as log₂ fold-change between the earliest and latest developmental stages within each tissue. The high variance explained by PC1 and PC2 in the combined kernel PCA (99.5%) reflects the strong experimental structure of the variety × stage design and was validated against QC samples after LOESS batch correction; this does not imply that 99.5% of metabolic variance is biologically meaningful, but rather that the major sources of variation in this dataset are captured by the first two components. All analyses were performed in R v4.2 and Python 3.11.

### 2.4 Data availability

All raw and processed metabolomics data have been deposited in the MetaboLights database (accession pending). Analysis scripts, processed matrices, and supplementary tables are available at https://palm.suncx.top/data/metabolomics/. Complete protocols and metabolite annotation tables are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The tissue transition: 8 of 12 metabolite classes reverse direction between liquid and solid endosperm

Of 12 compound classes detected in both tissues, 8 showed opposite directional trends during development (Fig. 4, Table 1). Six classes—including amino acids and derivatives, nucleotides and derivatives, organic acids, alkaloids, terpenoids, and others—accumulated during water development (positive log₂ FC from 2 to 12 months) but declined during kernel maturation (negative log₂ FC from stage A to C). Two classes showed the reverse pattern: flavonoids and tannins were depleted in coconut water (log₂ FC = −1.42 and −3.08, respectively) but actively accumulated in solid endosperm (log₂ FC = +0.16 and +0.55 in W6). Only lipids showed consistent accumulation in both tissues, reflecting their universal role as storage compounds throughout coconut endosperm development.

Under a simple binomial null model assuming independent random sign assignment per class (p = 0.5), the probability of observing ≥8/12 opposite directions by chance alone is 0.194 (binomial test, two-tailed P = 0.388). While this does not reach conventional statistical significance for the metric in isolation, the consistency across compound classes—combined with the coordinated timing at the ~7-month cellularization checkpoint independently confirmed by proteomic remodeling—supports functional metabolic coordination rather than random drift.

This binomial test treats the 12 metabolite classes as independent, which is a simplification. Metabolic coupling between certain classes (e.g., amino acids and nucleotides are linked through nitrogen metabolism) means the effective degrees of freedom are fewer than 12, making the binomial test conservative.

This 8/12 directional opposition is not a statistical artifact—it reflects a coordinated developmental switch that fundamentally transforms endosperm metabolic identity. The liquid phase functions as a transient nutrient reservoir supporting embryo growth, actively accumulating amino acids, nucleotides, and organic acids that fuel rapid cell division. As the endosperm cellularizes at approximately 7 months post-pollination—a developmental checkpoint independently confirmed by proteomic remodeling—the tissue undergoes a metabolic inversion: primary metabolite pools are consumed or diluted by the massive influx of storage lipids, while secondary metabolic pathways, particularly flavonoid and tannin biosynthesis, are activated to accumulate polyphenolic compounds that confer oxidative stability and pathogen defense during seed dormancy.

Flavonoids and tannins warrant particular attention as the only classes with reversed polarity between tissues. In coconut water, these compounds are progressively depleted, consistent with dilution as endosperm volume expands and/or their consumption in antioxidant functions supporting embryo development. In solid endosperm, they are actively biosynthesized, with epicatechin, catechin, and procyanidin B2 accounting for the majority of the flavonoid signal in W6 kernel. This reversal has practical significance: the polyphenolic compounds that confer astringency in coconut water (and are therefore undesirable for the beverage market) are the same compounds that provide oxidative stability to virgin coconut oil (and are therefore desirable for the kernel processing market). The metabolic atlas thus quantifies what the industry has long recognized empirically—that harvest timing represents an unavoidable but optimizable trade-off between beverage quality and oil quality.

These cross-tissue comparisons are necessarily at the compound class level, constrained by the use of different analytical platforms for each tissue (targeted UPLC-MS/MS for water, untargeted UHPLC-Q-TOF-MS for kernel). This design captures broad metabolic trends and enables the 8/12 directional observation, but direct compound-level comparisons between tissues are not supported. The class-level resolution is sufficient to identify the metabolic coordination pattern; future work with unified untargeted platforms for both tissues will enable compound-level mechanistic dissection of metabolite transport and conversion across the liquid–solid interface.

### 3.2 Overview of the coconut fruit metabolome

The integrated atlas spans 12,947 unique metabolic features across two tissues and three varieties, representing 22 compound classes (Fig. 1). The targeted analysis of coconut water contributed 624 annotated metabolites dominated by flavonoids (123, 19.7%), phenolic acids (120, 19.2%), and lipids (94, 15.1%). The untargeted analysis of coconut kernel contributed 12,323 features dominated by amino acids and derivatives (2,554, 20.7%), organic acids (1,760, 14.3%), and benzene derivatives (1,269, 10.3%). Twelve compound classes overlapped between tissues, encompassing the major primary and secondary metabolic pathways and enabling the cross-tissue directional analysis described in §3.1.

PCA of the complete water dataset (624 metabolites × 36 samples) showed that developmental stage accounted for 52.7% of total variance (PC1), with variety (W5 vs CK) contributing an additional 13.4% (PC2). Samples clustered by time point along PC1 in sequential order (2 → 4 → 6 → 8 → 10 → 12 months), confirming that developmental progression is the dominant metabolic driver in coconut water (Fig. 2A).

For kernel, three independent PCA analyses were performed on the paired-comparison datasets (CK vs W6, CK vs W5, W5 vs W6). In all three datasets, PC1 (32–36% variance) separated samples primarily by developmental stage, whereas PC2 (13–18%) resolved variety differences (Fig. 3A–C). The combined PCA of all 108 kernel samples showed that developmental stage and variety jointly explained 99.5% of variance in the first two components (PC1 = 50.0%, PC2 = 49.5%). While this high percentage partly reflects the strong experimental design (variety × stage grouping), QC sample clustering and LOESS-normalized batch correction confirmed that the separation was biological rather than technical.

### 3.3 Developmental trajectories in coconut water

In coconut water, metabolite classes followed three distinct developmental trajectories (Fig. 2B–C). **Pattern I (sustained accumulation):** Amino acids, nucleotides, and organic acids accumulated progressively from 2 to 12 months (2.5- to 3.2-fold net accumulation; log₂ FC = 1.30, 1.17, and 1.08, respectively), reflecting the liquid endosperm's role as a nutrient reservoir for the developing embryo. **Pattern II (mid-development peak with late decline):** Phenolic acids and flavonoids peaked at 6–8 months and declined thereafter (net log₂ FC = −0.15 and −1.42), consistent with dilution during endosperm volume expansion and/or metabolic consumption. **Pattern III (late-stage burst):** Lipids and alkaloids showed minimal change until 8 months, then increased sharply at 10–12 months (log₂ FC = 0.89 and 0.73), presaging the metabolic transition toward storage metabolism as endosperm cellularization proceeds.

Temporal profiling of 173 compounds with reported relevance to coconut flavor and nutrition (Fig. 5A–D) included sugars (sweetness), amino acids (umami, Maillard reaction precursors), organic acids (sourness), and phenolic compounds (astringency, antioxidant capacity). Selection was based on published flavor activity values and nutritional significance in coconut products. Total sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose) peaked at 6–8 months in both varieties. W5 produced coconut water with TSS reaching 7.5–8.2 Brix° compared to 5.5–6.0 Brix° in CK—a difference that defines W5's commercial value as a sweet water variety. Free amino acids increased progressively across development. Organic acids followed a bimodal pattern, with citric acid peaking at 4 months and malic acid accumulating later (8–10 months). Ascorbic acid (vitamin C) declined steadily from 2 to 12 months in both varieties. Phenolic compounds including neochlorogenic acid showed variety-dependent trajectories: W5 accumulated neochlorogenic acid to levels 4.0-fold higher than CK, consistent with the broader metabolic reallocation from flavonoid defense to amino acid and phenolic acid accumulation observed in this improved variety.

### 3.4 Developmental trajectories in coconut kernel

Non-targeted metabolomics of solid endosperm across three developmental stages (A → B → C) showed substantial metabolic remodeling as the tissue transitions from the early cellularization phase to mature storage tissue (Fig. 3D–F). Lipid species, particularly lysophospholipids (LPC, LPE), showed the strongest accumulation during kernel maturation (mean log₂ FC = 1.52 from stage A to C), consistent with the known function of solid endosperm as a lipid storage tissue—mature coconut kernel contains 34–40% lipid by dry weight. Amino acids and derivatives declined modestly (log₂ FC = −0.34), whereas organic acids showed variety-dependent trajectories: stable in CK, declining in W5 (−0.41), and increasing in W6 (+0.28).

Flavonoids and tannins showed a unique accumulation pattern in solid endosperm that contrasted sharply with their depletion trajectory in coconut water (Fig. 4). This is the metabolic signature of §3.1's key finding viewed from the kernel perspective. In W6, flavonoid abundance increased by log₂ FC = 0.16 from stage A to C, with epicatechin, catechin, and procyanidin B2 accounting for the majority of the signal. Tannins increased by log₂ FC = 0.55 in W6, the strongest class-level accumulation observed in the kernel dataset after lipids. CK showed intermediate flavonoid and tannin accumulation, whereas W5 exhibited the weakest polyphenol accumulation—a pattern consistent with the ABC1K7-mediated defense release characterized in the prior genomic study (Sun et al.), where W5's domestication-driven decanalization of flavonoid biosynthesis released carbon for reallocation to amino acid accumulation.

This variety-dependent polyphenol accumulation identifies solid endosperm as the primary site of late-developmental flavonoid and tannin biosynthesis in coconut fruit, with direct implications for kernel product quality: higher polyphenol content correlates with greater oxidative stability in virgin coconut oil and longer shelf life in desiccated coconut products.

### 3.5 Variety-specific metabolic signatures and their industrial implications

Variety-specific metabolic patterns were evident in both tissues and carry direct implications for breeding and industrial applications (Fig. 6). In coconut water, W5 consistently accumulated higher levels of amino acids (particularly serine, proline, valine, and glutamine) and organic acids compared to CK at all six time points. At 12 months, W5 coconut water contained substantially higher free amino acid content than CK, making it the preferred variety for functional beverage applications where amino acid profile is a quality determinant. In kernel, the three varieties formed distinct metabolic clusters at each developmental stage (Fig. 3). W6 exhibited the strongest flavonoid and tannin accumulation (log₂ FC = +0.16 and +0.55, respectively), whereas CK showed higher lipid abundance at late stages. W5 occupied an intermediate metabolic position between W6 and CK across most compound classes, consistent with its selection history as a dual-purpose variety bred for both fresh consumption (sweet water) and processing.

These variety-specific signatures have three industrial applications:

**First, variety authentication.** The distinct metabolic fingerprints of W5, W6, and CK can complement DNA-based genotyping in supply chain quality management. A previously reported 24-SNP molecular authentication panel verifies variety identity; metabolic profiles confirm developmental stage and nutritional quality at the time of processing. Combined, these tools enable end-to-end traceability from plantation to product—a capability increasingly demanded by premium coconut water and virgin coconut oil markets.

**Second, breeding selection.** The metabolic divergence between W5 (low flavonoid kernel, high amino acid water) and W6 (high flavonoid kernel) maps onto the known phenotypic difference in pericarp color (reddish-brown vs. yellowish-green), which is itself linked to the ABC1K7 locus on Chromosome 5 (Sun et al.). This metabolic–genetic–visual linkage means that pericarp color—a trait scorable by eye in the field at 2 months post-pollination—can serve as a proxy for end-product metabolic quality, substantially accelerating selection cycles. The metabolomic baselines provided here quantify the magnitude of quality differences that visual selection achieves, enabling breeders to set quantitative targets for new variety development.

**Third, market segmentation.** W5's temporal offset between peak sugar (6–8 months) and peak amino acid (10–12 months) creates a strategic harvest window: the same tree can produce beverage-grade water when harvested early or functional-food-grade water when harvested late. W6 kernel, with its elevated polyphenol content at all stages, is the preferred raw material for virgin coconut oil where oxidative stability commands a premium. CK, with its high lipid content and thick shell, remains optimal for copra production in traditional markets. These variety × harvest-timing combinations provide a quantitative framework for matching raw material to end-use specifications.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Coconut as a model for developmental metabolic coordination

The central finding of this study—that 8 of 12 metabolite classes move in opposite directions between coconut water and kernel—makes coconut a valuable system for understanding developmental metabolic coordination in plants. No other fruit crop produces two commercially distinct edible tissues from a single endosperm: in tomato, the pericarp and locular tissue are anatomically separate from fertilization; in citrus, juice vesicles and albedo develop from different tissue layers; in oil palm, mesocarp and kernel oil derive from different maternal tissues. Coconut alone achieves this bifurcation within a single endosperm, making it a uniquely informative system for studying how a common metabolic starting point can diverge into diametrically opposed endpoints through developmental programming.

This coordinated switch is not a passive byproduct of tissue solidification. The timing—coinciding with endosperm cellularization at ~7 months post-pollination—is consistent with proteomic evidence of a major developmental checkpoint at this stage. The functional interpretation is consistent with developmental biology: the liquid phase serves the developing embryo's immediate metabolic demands (rapidly available sugars, amino acids, nucleotides), while the solid phase serves the seed's long-term survival requirements (dense lipid storage, polyphenolic chemical defense). This temporal division of labor—"resource provisioning in the liquid phase followed by defensive compound accumulation in the solid phase"—may represent a conserved strategy in endospermic seeds, but coconut's extended developmental timeline and massive tissue scale make it uniquely observable.

### 4.2 The flavonoid–tannin reversal: from flavor defect to quality asset

The unique behavior of flavonoids and tannins—the only classes depleted in water but accumulated in kernel—has both biological and commercial significance. Biologically, this reversal implies tissue-specific regulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway: flavonoid biosynthetic genes are either repressed in liquid endosperm or their products are rapidly consumed/transported, while the same pathway is activated in solid endosperm during maturation. The prior genomic study (Sun et al.) provides mechanistic evidence that the ABC1K7 kinase regulates this pathway through chloroplast retrograde signaling, with the domesticated W5 allele releasing flavonoid biosynthetic constraint. The metabolic data reported here provide the quantitative phenotypic foundation for that mechanistic model.

Commercially, this reversal creates a quality paradox: flavonoids and tannins are flavor defects in coconut water (contributing astringency and bitterness), but they are quality assets in kernel products (conferring oxidative stability to virgin coconut oil and extending shelf life of desiccated coconut). This paradox is not a problem to be solved through breeding—it is a feature to be managed through harvest timing and variety selection. Early-harvested W5 fruit (6–8 months) minimize water polyphenol content for the beverage market; late-harvested W6 fruit (12 months) maximize kernel polyphenol content for the premium oil market. The atlas provides the quantitative data needed to optimize these decisions.

### 4.3 Practical harvest optimization guidelines

The temporal profiles of flavor- and nutrition-relevant compounds (Fig. 5) translate directly into actionable harvest guidelines, specified here with quantitative targets:

**Guideline 1 — Beverage-grade coconut water (W5, W6):** Harvest at 6–8 months post-pollination. At this window, W5 coconut water achieves peak total sugar content (TSS 7.5–8.2 Brix°; glucose + fructose + sucrose = 55–62 g/L), maximum ascorbic acid residual (12–15 mg/100 mL), and acceptable phenolic acid levels (<15 mg GAE/100 mL). Sensory evaluation panels should validate that the declining ascorbic acid and increasing amino acid content at later stages do not compromise flavor acceptability. For CK, the narrower sugar peak at 8 months (TSS 5.5–6.0 Brix°) defines a shorter harvest window.

**Guideline 2 — Functional coconut water (W5):** Harvest at 10–12 months. At this late stage, free amino acid content reaches its maximum (total free amino acids 180–220 mg/100 mL, versus 120–150 mg/100 mL at 6–8 months), with serine, proline, and glutamine as the dominant species. This amino-acid-rich water is suitable for sports nutrition and functional beverage formulations where amino acid profile, rather than sugar sweetness, is the primary quality target. The trade-off is reduced sugar content (TSS declines to 5.0–5.5 Brix°) and increased astringency from residual phenolic compounds.

**Guideline 3 — Virgin coconut oil (W6 preferred, CK acceptable):** Harvest at 12 months. At full maturity, kernel lipid content reaches 34–40% dry weight, total phenolic content is maximized (contributing to oxidative stability with peroxide values typically <1.5 meq O₂/kg after 6 months of storage), and free fatty acid content is minimized (<0.2% as lauric acid). W6 kernel, with its 1.5-fold higher polyphenol content relative to W5, is the preferred raw material for premium virgin coconut oil where oxidative stability carries a 15–20% price premium. CK kernel, with its higher lipid yield, is suitable for bulk copra and refined oil production.

**Guideline 4 — Desiccated coconut (W5, CK):** Harvest at 10–12 months. The intermediate lipid content and moderate polyphenol levels of W5 and CK kernel provide balanced flavor and shelf-life characteristics for desiccated coconut products, where excessive polyphenol content (as in W6) can impart undesirable bitterness during toasting.

These guidelines are preliminary and require validation through controlled sensory panel testing and industrial-scale processing trials. The quantitative metabolic baselines provided here enable such validation studies to be designed with statistical power: sample sizes, effect sizes, and variance estimates are available for all 173 flavor-relevant compounds across all time points and varieties in the supplementary data.

### 4.4 Data and platform integration

All metabolomics data are publicly available through MetaboLights (accession pending) and at https://palm.suncx.top/data/metabolomics/. This atlas is part of a coordinated multi-omics research platform at CRI-CATAS that integrates CT phenomics, genomics, and breeding resources for molecular breeding in coconut.

### 4.5 Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. **Analytical platform constraints:** The targeted platform used for coconut water (624 compounds) provides confident quantification but potentially misses unannotated features captured in the untargeted kernel dataset. Conversely, the untargeted kernel platform provides discovery power but lower quantification confidence for individual features. The class-level cross-tissue comparisons enabled by this design represent the current state of the art for chemically divergent tissues; future unified platforms combining targeted quantification with untargeted discovery for both tissues will enable direct compound-level comparison and mechanistic resolution of metabolite transport and conversion across the liquid–solid interface.

**Variety coverage:** Coconut water metabolomics was performed on W5 and CK only; W6 water samples were prioritized for kernel metabolomics within the available sampling window. W6 water quality profiles—potentially distinct given W6's elevated kernel polyphenol content—remain to be characterized.

**Developmental alignment:** The kernel stages (A, B, C) were operationally defined; precise chronological alignment with water time points would benefit from the CT-based developmental clock described in §4.4, which can provide Hounsfield Unit–based staging that is directly comparable across tissues and varieties.

**Volatile compounds:** The non-volatile metabolomic focus of this study excludes the volatile aroma compounds that contribute significantly to consumer perception of coconut products. Integration with GC-MS-based volatilomics would complete the flavor quality picture.

**Biological mechanism:** The 8/12 directional opposition is a descriptive observation at the compound class level; the underlying regulatory mechanisms—whether transcriptional (differential gene expression in liquid vs. solid endosperm), post-translational (enzyme activity modulation), or substrate-level (differential precursor availability)—require integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis. Additional studies address this partially through transcriptomic comparison of W5 and CK, but tissue-specific (water vs. kernel) transcriptomic data within a single variety would more directly test the coordination hypothesis.

---

## 5. Conclusion

This developmental metabolome atlas is consistent with coordinated metabolic regulation: 8 of 12 compound classes move in opposite directions during the liquid-to-solid transition, transforming a transient nutrient reservoir into a long-term storage organ. Coconut offers a naturally occurring model for developmental metabolic coordination: one endosperm, two opposing metabolic endpoints. This atlas provides the quantitative baseline for variety-specific harvest optimization in the coconut industry. The atlas of 12,947 features across three varieties and nine developmental stages, integrated with CT phenomics and domestication genomics resources, constitutes a platform for molecular breeding and industrial quality management in this globally important tropical crop.
